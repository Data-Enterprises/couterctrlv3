Item Actions

Desktop · one store, one week, a list of items
What it does

Takes a list of items that need attention and gets you to the one call worth making on each. Every row compares what sold against what was delivered, so a stocking problem and a demand problem stop looking alike. Each item comes back with a suggested next step and the evidence behind it:

  • Reorder — the shelf emptied mid-week, or the last delivery has all sold, or nothing has come in for over two weeks.
  • Call vendor — cost that will not hold still: repeated changes, a rise that stayed up, a cost bouncing around. Each finding is dated.
  • Reprice — ringing below cost. Either the tag itself is wrong, or a promotion is underwater, with the days it has run and the money given away.
  • Investigate — in stock, priced right, still not moving.
  • No action — flagged upstream, but it holds up here.
  • Insufficient — not enough on file to say anything, said plainly rather than guessed at.
How you use it

Pick a store, a week, and the items. The items can be typed, pasted, or uploaded as the file that Sub Dept Margins and Vendors already export — so the usual route is to spot the flagged departments on a performance page, export them, and hand that file straight to this page.

The report appears first and the delivery history fills in behind it, because sales are quick to read and deliveries are not. Clicking a row opens a panel for that one item: its deliveries, the prices the register actually charged, and the individual transactions.

Where the numbers come from

Two sources, both already in CounterCtrl. Sales come from the weekly item and sub-department figures — the same numbers behind Sub Dept Margins, so a row here agrees with the page you came from. Deliveries come from received invoices, read back 90 days whatever week you picked, so "never received" means genuinely absent rather than outside your dates. Register-level prices for a single item are fetched only when you open that item.

Why it works this way

It suggests, it does not conclude. The data can point at a likely cause; confirming it is still a person's job, and a tool that promises more than that loses the trust it runs on.

Units, not dollars. Sales being down can mean fewer sold or a lower price. Units being down can only mean fewer left the shelf.

Stores whose orders never reach us still get graded. Orders received electronically do not come through the scan this delivery data is built from, so a store can trade normally and show no invoices at all. Those items used to land in one unhelpful pile. They are now judged on what sales alone can prove — price against cost, and trend — and only the two suggestions that genuinely need a delivery trail are withheld. Where no invoice exists, cost falls back to the week's average and the row says so.

It cannot see stock on hand. Nothing in the data reports an opening count, and shrink, damage and transfers are invisible to it, so the delivery column reads as a change rather than a count. The page states that rather than implying an inventory it does not have.

Sales Tracker

Desktop · one store or a group
What it does

Tracks every sub department against the same weeks last year, as a row of week cards you can open to see the days inside. Sales this year and last, the gap in dollars and in percent, and average transaction size beside them.

How you use it

Search a store or a group and a date range. Each week becomes a card; each department is a row you can expand for the individual days. Green is ahead of last year, red is behind. The whole thing exports.

Average transaction size answers the question the totals raise: whether a change came from more baskets or bigger ones.

Where the numbers come from

Day-level sub-department totals, asked for as an explicit list of dates rather than a range — this year's days and their partners from last year in the same request. Both sides are net sales counted the same way, so the two figures are comparable.

Why it works this way

Same weekday, not same date. A Saturday is always measured against a Saturday, because a calendar-date comparison puts a weekend against a Tuesday and calls the difference a trend. Holidays are matched to the real holiday last year instead, and where that means two days share one partner, the day is marked with a star rather than quietly counted twice.

A missing day reads as a dash, not a zero. Where last year has no matching day at all, those days are left out of the totals on both sides — otherwise a store that opened mid-year shows a collapse that never happened.

No grading here. The tracker reports movement and leaves the judgement to you. Severity rankings live on the performance pages, where a threshold you set decides them.

Client Help Desk

Desktop · two views, decided by who you are
What it does

Support tickets inside CounterCtrl, with the whole conversation on each one. As a client you see your own company's tickets and the replies on them. As a support tech you see the queue across every company, plus the internal notes that clients never see.

How you use it

Three columns in the order you use them: the list picks a ticket, the middle is the conversation, and the right-hand panel is who sent it. Status and priority are the first things anyone asks about a ticket, so they sit in its header.

You can file a ticket from any page, and the page you were on travels with it — including a screenshot, attached before the ticket ever opens, so support sees what you saw. An open ticket updates itself every few seconds. The queue is politer about it: the Refresh button lights up and says there is new activity, and pressing it applies what has already arrived.

Where the numbers come from

Its own tables: the tickets, their comments, their attachments, and the support-tech roster. Which company a ticket belongs to and who opened it are resolved from the user records at the time you ask, so a ticket follows the person rather than carrying a stale copy of their details.

Why it works this way

Which view you get is the server's answer, not the screen's guess. The rule lives with the data, and internal notes are filtered out before they are ever sent — not hidden once they arrive. A ticket outside your scope is not refused, it simply does not exist for you, so nobody learns what they cannot see.

Four statuses, not three — open, in progress, waiting, closed. "Waiting" is the one that was missing, and it is the honest state for a ticket that is back with the client.

Attachments are handed over only when opened. A file gets a time-limited link at the moment you click it; nothing hands out links for files nobody opens.

Still internal while we test

These work. They also write real files, touch money, or name a person — so they stay with our own accounts until every question a client could ask about them has an answer.

Sales Export

Desktop · builds a file you download
What it does

Builds a sales file to your own specification rather than to a report's. Pick the stores and dates, choose which columns go in it and which lines to keep, and the file is written and handed back as a download. Either line by line, or summarised — grouped by whatever you choose, with totals beside the groups.

How you use it

Search a store or group and a range. The page then describes your own data back to you: which stores the search actually covered, which sale types occur in that window, every column available, and ten sample rows. Every list you narrow by is built from that answer, so there is nothing to pick that is not really there.

Narrowing happens on screen, so the sample redraws as you tick. When it looks right, build the file — and save the setup as a named configuration you can reload later onto any range. Previous Builds keeps every file you have made alongside the exact question that produced it: the dates, the stores, the row count, and the download.

Where the numbers come from

The sales detail table itself — one row per register line, with about a hundred columns on it. This is the layer underneath every sales report in the app, which is what makes the page useful and also what makes the notes below necessary. The file is written straight from the database to secure storage and downloaded from there, so the data never travels through the app. Saved configurations sit with your own saved work, and a configuration that is not yours cannot be loaded.

Why it works this way

A total from this file will not match a sales report, and that is correct. Raw register lines include tender rows, voids, department transfers and untendered modifications, and refunds are stored as positive numbers — so a store total taken from here runs to roughly double what the weekly sales page reports for the same window. Each summarised column is named after the operation that produced it, so a file cannot quietly be read as a report. Anything that has to reconcile against a report should start from the report.

Personal columns are marked, and their sample values stay off the screen. Customer name, address, phone and the rest are flagged in the picker, and the preview shows the column without showing a real person's details — a preview gets read over someone's shoulder far more often than a file gets opened. The file you build contains the real values.

The preview is capped at 31 days; the export is not. Describing your data means reading every line in the window, so the preview's cost follows the range while it hands back the same ten sample rows either way. Get the setup right on a month, then run it on whatever range you need.

A build is a record, not a bookmark. Configurations are meant to be renamed and edited; a build is the question exactly as it was asked that day, written beside the file and never rewritten — which is what makes a file from six weeks ago explainable.

Price Opt — Sub Department, and Price Opt — Vendor

Desktop · one store at a time · two ways into the same analysis
What it does

Shows one item's price points two ways: the prices you would estimate from daily sales totals, and the prices the register actually charged, side by side. The gap between the two columns is the finding — a price on the estimated side with no counterpart on the actual side never existed. A suggested price sits underneath, read off the actual side only.

How you use it

Two pages, because there are two ways people look for the item. Sub Department opens a department and lists the items in it, loading nothing until you open one. Vendor groups the same items by who supplies them. From the item rightward the two are identical.

The vendor page has more to do up front: the supplier is recorded on the item line rather than on the department, so every department has to be read before a single vendor can be named. That is why it is a separate page with an honest two-step loading message, instead of a toggle that quietly costs forty times as much.

Where the numbers come from

The estimated side uses the daily department and item roll-ups the performance pages already read. The actual side goes to the receipts: the item's own transactions are located by barcode and then opened, up to a few hundred of them, and the page tells you when it has capped that rather than pretending it read everything.

Why it works this way

Only the register gets a vote. An estimate divides a day's dollars by a day's units, so nineteen sales at $13.99 and one at $12.99 produce a price of $13.88 that nobody was ever charged. That is fine as an observation and disqualifying as a recommendation, so the suggestion ignores it.

One store at a time, like the margin pages this sits beneath. Price points are decided per store, and averaging them across a group is how a real problem in one store disappears.

AWG Invoices

Desktop · reads a file you already receive
What it does

Reads AWG's electronic invoice file and shows what it says — every invoice in the file, every line on the invoice, with cost, retail, allowances, cases and margin. Each invoice is checked against its own printed totals before you are shown a single figure.

How you use it

Open the file. Each invoice becomes a card that says Reconciled or Did not reconcile, and you can filter straight to the failures. If a line was misread you can correct the cell, and the totals and all four checks re-run on the spot.

Where the numbers come from

Nothing but the file, and it never leaves the computer. The invoice is decoded in the browser: no upload, no database, no copy kept. Close the tab and it is gone.

Why it works this way

Four checks, to the cent. Cost, extended retail, allowances and cases are each added up from the detail lines and compared to the invoice's own summary. All four matching is the only proof the page offers that the file was read correctly, which is why it sits above the numbers rather than in a footnote. Where a check fails, the card names which one and by how much — an invoice that does not add up should not be acted on or passed along.

Money is held as whole cents. Ordinary decimal arithmetic drifts by fractions of a cent across a few hundred lines, which is enough to make a perfectly good invoice fail its own reconciliation. Nothing is rounded here until it is printed on screen.

Margin is worked out from the lines, not read from any reported field, because a margin that disagrees with the two numbers printed beside it would be worse than none. Fields the format does not carry are left blank rather than shown as zero.

LP Actions

Desktop · one store or a group · loss prevention
What it does

Takes every exception type at a store — voids, no sales, refunds, the rest — and judges each one against its own recent history rather than against other stores. The question is what changed, not what is highest: a lane that always voids eleven times a week is not news, and one that went from four to forty is.

Each type comes back as Investigate, Watch or Steady. Open one and the cashiers behind it are ranked by how far each moved against their own weekly normal; open a cashier and you get their case — the pattern by hour and by week, the items involved, and the receipts themselves.

How you use it

Pick a store or group and a week-ending date. Four weeks are read: the week being graded, plus three of history to compare it against. Add week extends that, up to a quarter. Adding a week re-grades the whole page rather than just adding a column, because a wider history changes every verdict on it.

Where the numbers come from

Register exception lines — the same receipt-level data behind the existing Loss Prevention screens. One pass establishes which exception types occurred in your scope at all, then each week is read in a single request: every line carries its own store, cashier and exception type, so one call per week covers every combination the page shows. Receipts are opened only when you ask for one.

Why it works this way

It is deliberately not a leaderboard. Ranking by volume returns the busiest operator every single time, because the busiest operator has the most of everything. Ranking by movement against a person's own normal is the only version of this list worth a manager's morning.

Thin weeks are left alone. Under five occurrences in the latest week, a type stays Steady no matter what the percentage says — two becoming six is a 200% rise and no information.

A baseline widens as you add history, so a spike can be downgraded once there is more to compare it against. That is the point of asking for more weeks, and it is why the page would rather show a verdict that can move than one that looks certain.

Why this one is still internal: the grading rule is settled, and the case file behind an individual cashier is still being built out. Putting a name in front of a manager is the highest-consequence thing this app does, so it stays with us until that evidence is complete.

Suggested Order Weight

Scale departments — anything sold by the pound
What it does

Suggests how many pounds to order for a by-the-pound department, from what it has really been selling on each day of the week, how long an order takes to arrive, and how many days it has to cover. It shows its working: the weekday rates it used, ordered against sold over the lookback, and the items that have stopped selling.

Where it stands

The page is finished and kept out of the menu entirely, because the calculation behind it is only deployed to our development environment. It appears the day that ships — nothing about the page itself needs to change.
